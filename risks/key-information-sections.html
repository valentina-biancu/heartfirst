Nine things to know about plaque

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above — crawlable, shareable, and worth reading before your next health team conversation.

What plaque actually is

Plaque is a build-up inside the artery wall — not a lump floating in blood. It can contain cholesterol-rich particles, immune cells, fibrous tissue, and calcium deposits.

Where it forms

Plaque develops within the artery wall itself, starting at the inner lining. The blood channel may not narrow noticeably for years, which is why symptoms can be absent even as plaque grows.

Why structure matters

A plaque has layers: a lipid-rich core, a fibrous cap covering it, and often an inflammatory environment around it. How these layers behave — not just how large the plaque is — shapes risk.

Plaque can grow silently

Arteries have some ability to remodel as plaque builds, delaying significant narrowing. This means plaque can progress for years without producing symptoms or flagging on routine tests.

Size is not the whole story

A smaller, unstable plaque with a thin fibrous cap may carry more immediate risk than a large, stable, well-calcified plaque. The biological state of the plaque matters alongside its size.

Plaque is the starting point for a cascade

Plaque becomes especially important when it connects with inflammation, fibrous cap rupture, and clot formation — the sequence covered in the next Risk Atlas modules.

Not just "blocked pipes"

Arteries are living tissue. Plaque is part of an active biological process involving lipids, immune cells, mechanical stress, and repair responses — not a simple blockage.

Normal cholesterol does not mean no plaque

Standard cholesterol results do not show artery-wall plaque directly. A person with a normal LDL-C result can still have significant plaque burden, particularly if ApoB or Lp(a) are elevated.

Lifestyle helps — but context still matters

Lifestyle changes can affect modifiable risk factors. They do not replace clinical assessment of plaque-related risk, particularly when inherited factors such as elevated Lp(a) are involved.

Nine things to know about inflammation and heart risk

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above — crawlable, shareable, and worth reading before your next health team conversation.

What cardiovascular inflammation is

In the cardiovascular context, inflammation is a chronic biological state within and around artery walls — not a visible or acute response. It can be present without symptoms or obvious signs.

Where it matters most

Inflammation is particularly significant at the fibrous cap — the cover over a plaque's lipid core. Inflammatory activity can thin and weaken the cap, which connects directly to rupture risk.

How Lp(a) is involved

Lp(a) carries oxidised phospholipids with pro-inflammatory properties. Elevated Lp(a) can amplify the inflammatory environment around a plaque independently of other risk factors.

hs-CRP is useful but non-specific

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein can reflect systemic inflammation. It can also be elevated for many reasons unrelated to cardiovascular risk — it is most useful when interpreted alongside lipid and Lp(a) results.

Inflammation and plaque are distinct processes

Plaque buildup and inflammatory activity are related but separate. Both can be present at once, and each can amplify the effect of the other on cardiovascular risk.

Statins have anti-inflammatory effects too

Statins reduce cardiovascular risk partly through mechanisms beyond LDL-C lowering — including anti-inflammatory effects. The JUPITER trial showed benefit in people with elevated hs-CRP even when LDL-C was not high.

Inflammation is not the same as infection

Cardiovascular inflammation is a chronic, often silent biological state — not a swollen joint, fever, or acute illness. It can be present without any noticeable signs for years.

A normal hs-CRP does not rule out plaque activity

Lp(a)-associated inflammatory biology can contribute to plaque activity even when hs-CRP is within the normal range. Inflammation at the plaque level is not always reflected in systemic blood markers.

Reducing inflammation is not simple

No single intervention reliably eliminates cardiovascular inflammation. Risk management involves addressing the broader picture: lipids, blood pressure, metabolic risk, Lp(a), and lifestyle — in combination.

Nine things to know about plaque rupture

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above — crawlable, shareable, and worth reading before your next health team conversation.

What a fibrous cap is

The fibrous cap is a layer of tissue that forms over a plaque's lipid core, separating the soft interior from the flowing blood. Its integrity is central to plaque stability.

What rupture means

Plaque rupture — more precisely, fibrous cap rupture — is when the cap breaks, exposing the lipid-rich core and triggering the blood's clotting response at the rupture site.

Why the cap can break

Inflammatory activity can degrade the fibrous cap tissue, making it thinner and more vulnerable to mechanical stress. This is the link between inflammation and acute cardiovascular events.

Rupture is not inevitable

Many plaques, including some that are large and lipid-rich, remain stable throughout a person's life. Rupture is one possible outcome — not a guaranteed progression.

Rupture triggers clot formation

When plaque contents are exposed to flowing blood, the coagulation cascade activates. A clot forms at the rupture site — which may partially or fully block the artery. That is the next module.

Lp(a) plays a role here too

Elevated Lp(a) is associated with both pro-inflammatory plaque biology and pro-thrombotic pathways — meaning it can contribute to both cap vulnerability and the clotting response that follows rupture.

Rupture cannot be predicted by blood tests alone

No standard blood test directly measures fibrous cap integrity or identifies a plaque at imminent risk of rupture. Clinical assessment, imaging, and risk-factor management are all needed for a full picture.

The biggest plaque is not always the most dangerous

Rupture risk depends on plaque biology — including cap thickness and inflammatory state — not just on how much an artery is narrowed. A smaller, inflamed, thin-capped plaque can carry significant risk.

Events can feel sudden even when risk has built for years

Because plaque can grow silently and rupture without warning symptoms, cardiovascular events can appear sudden even when underlying disease has been developing for a long time.

Nine things to know about clot formation and heart risk

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above — crawlable, shareable, and worth reading before your next health team conversation.

What triggers a cardiovascular clot

In the cardiovascular context, a clot most commonly forms when the contents of a ruptured plaque are exposed to flowing blood. The blood's clotting system — the coagulation cascade — responds to the exposed material.

How a clot can reduce blood flow

A clot that forms at a rupture site can partially or fully block the artery lumen. Partial blockage can cause reduced flow and symptoms; full blockage can cut off blood supply completely.

Platelets and fibrin — the key components

Clot formation involves platelets sticking to the exposed site and fibrin strands forming a mesh around them. This is why antiplatelet medications (such as aspirin) and anticoagulants work through different but related pathways.

Lp(a) has a pro-thrombotic biology

Lp(a) shares structural similarity with plasminogen — a protein involved in dissolving clots. Elevated Lp(a) may inhibit the body's natural clot-dissolving process, making clots more persistent.

Not every clot causes a heart attack or stroke

Some clots are partial, dissolve naturally, or stabilise without causing a complete blockage. The outcome depends on clot size, location, blood flow, and the body's own response — not a single binary event.

This is the hand-off point to acute events

Clot formation is where the plaque, inflammation, and rupture pathway becomes time-sensitive. The next atlas modules — Heart attack and Stroke — show what can follow when blood flow to heart muscle or brain is cut off.

Clotting is not always dangerous

Clotting is a normal, essential biological process. The problem in cardiovascular disease is clotting at the wrong place — inside an artery at a plaque rupture site — rather than clotting itself.

Antiplatelet drugs and anticoagulants are not the same

Antiplatelet medications (such as aspirin or clopidogrel) affect platelet function. Anticoagulants affect the fibrin-forming coagulation cascade. Both can reduce clot-related risk but through different mechanisms.

A clot is not always preceded by obvious symptoms

The events leading to clot formation — plaque rupture, exposed contents, platelet activation — can happen without warning. This is why managing upstream risk factors matters before a clot event occurs.

Nine things to know about the cardiovascular cascade

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above — crawlable, shareable, and worth reading before your next health team conversation.

What the cascade is

The cardiovascular cascade is the connected sequence: plaque builds in the artery wall, inflammation makes it more active, the fibrous cap can rupture, a clot forms, and blood flow is reduced or cut off.

Each step creates the conditions for the next

No single step is inevitable, but each can make the next more likely. Plaque alone does not cause a heart attack — it is when plaque connects to rupture and clot that acute risk becomes immediate.

Where Lp(a) fits in the cascade

Lp(a) can contribute at multiple points: it promotes plaque formation, adds to inflammatory biology, and may inhibit the natural dissolution of clots — making it a multi-stage amplifier of cascade risk.

Risk factors stack and interact

ApoB, Lp(a), blood pressure, metabolic risk, smoking, and inflammation do not add up simply — they interact. A person with three moderate risk factors may carry more risk than someone with one severe factor alone.

The cascade can be interrupted at multiple points

Different interventions work at different steps: statins and lifestyle at the plaque stage, anti-inflammatory approaches at the inflammation stage, antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy at the clot stage.

The cascade connects to two separate events

The same sequence — plaque, inflammation, rupture, clot — can lead to a heart attack (via coronary arteries) or a stroke (via cerebral or carotid arteries), depending on where it occurs.

Not a single guaranteed sequence

The cascade is an educational framework, not a fixed biological script. Many people with plaque never progress to rupture. Many ruptures do not cause complete blockages. Risk is probabilistic, not deterministic.

The event can feel sudden even when the cascade is slow

The plaque-to-event process can span years or decades. The acute event — rupture and clot — can happen in minutes. This is why underlying risk matters even when nothing feels wrong.

Knowing the cascade helps frame the right questions

Understanding the cascade helps you ask better questions: not just "is my cholesterol normal?" but "what does my full risk picture show across plaque, inflammation, Lp(a), blood pressure, and family history?"

Nine things to know about heart attack

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above. If symptoms suggest a cardiac emergency, stop reading and seek urgent medical help immediately.

What a heart attack is

A heart attack — myocardial infarction — occurs when blood flow through a coronary artery is severely reduced or cut off, depriving heart muscle of oxygen. It is a medical emergency.

The most common cause

Most heart attacks are caused by plaque rupture in a coronary artery, triggering clot formation that blocks blood flow — not by gradual narrowing alone. The cascade from plaque to event can happen rapidly.

Why time is critical

Heart muscle begins to die within minutes of losing its blood supply. The sooner blood flow is restored, the less damage occurs. This is why calling emergency services immediately is essential.

Symptoms are not always dramatic

Heart attack symptoms can include chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to the arm, jaw, or back, breathlessness, nausea, and cold sweating. They can also be subtle — particularly in women and people with diabetes.

Lp(a) and coronary artery disease

Elevated Lp(a) is an independent risk factor for coronary artery disease and heart attack. It can contribute to plaque formation, plaque instability, and the pro-thrombotic environment that follows rupture.

ApoB matters alongside LDL-C

ApoB is a more direct measure of atherogenic particle burden than LDL-C alone. A person with normal LDL-C but elevated ApoB and Lp(a) may have a meaningfully elevated coronary risk that standard testing does not fully capture.

Normal results do not guarantee safety

A normal cholesterol panel, blood pressure reading, or ECG does not rule out coronary artery disease or the risk of a future event — particularly when ApoB, Lp(a), or family history have not been assessed.

Heart attack is not the same as cardiac arrest

A heart attack is a blocked artery causing muscle damage. Cardiac arrest is when the heart stops beating — a different emergency. A heart attack can lead to cardiac arrest, but they are not the same event.

Prevention is possible — but requires knowing your risk

Many heart attacks are considered preventable when risk is identified and acted on early. That requires knowing your full risk picture — not just standard cholesterol, but ApoB, Lp(a), blood pressure, metabolic health, and family history.

Nine things to know about stroke

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above. If symptoms suggest a stroke, stop reading and call emergency services immediately.

What a stroke is

A stroke occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is cut off, causing brain cells to begin dying within minutes. It is a medical emergency that requires immediate treatment.

The most common type

Ischaemic stroke — caused by a blocked artery — accounts for the majority of strokes. It shares the same upstream cascade as heart attack: plaque, inflammation, rupture, and clot, in the arteries supplying the brain.

Recognise the signs — FAST

Face drooping on one side. Arm weakness — one arm that cannot be raised. Speech that is slurred, confused, or absent. Time — call emergency services immediately. Do not wait to see if symptoms improve.

TIA is a warning that must be taken seriously

A transient ischaemic attack — TIA — produces stroke-like symptoms that resolve within minutes or hours. It should never be dismissed. A TIA is a warning of significantly elevated near-term stroke risk.

Lp(a) and stroke risk

Elevated Lp(a) is associated with increased risk of ischaemic stroke, particularly through its contributions to arterial plaque, pro-inflammatory biology, and the pro-thrombotic environment that follows plaque rupture.

Stroke risk shares its upstream factors with heart attack

ApoB, Lp(a), blood pressure, metabolic risk, atrial fibrillation, and family history all contribute to stroke risk through the same plaque and clot pathways. Managing these factors reduces risk for both events.

Stroke does not only happen in old age

Stroke can occur at any age. Elevated Lp(a), familial hypercholesterolaemia, and other inherited risk factors can cause arterial disease in younger people who have no other obvious risk factors.

Symptoms that improve are still emergencies

Symptoms that resolve on their own — a TIA — are not a reason to wait. The hours after a TIA carry among the highest stroke risk. Immediate assessment can prevent the next, potentially disabling, event.

Stroke and heart attack prevention overlap significantly

Most of the interventions that reduce heart attack risk — lipid management, blood pressure control, antiplatelet therapy, Lp(a) awareness — also reduce stroke risk. The pathways are different but the upstream causes are largely shared.

Nine things to know about aortic valve calcification

What the aortic valve does

The aortic valve sits between the left ventricle and the aorta. It opens to let blood out with each heartbeat and closes to prevent backflow. It has three leaflets that need to move freely to work properly.

What valve calcification is

Calcification is the build-up of calcium deposits on the valve leaflets. It makes the leaflets stiffer, thicker, and less able to open fully — reducing the flow of blood out of the heart with each beat.

Why Lp(a) is specifically associated with valve disease

Elevated Lp(a) is one of the most strongly established risk factors for aortic valve calcification. This is a separate pathway from Lp(a)'s role in artery plaque — it involves calcium deposition on valve tissue specifically.

Valve calcification is distinct from artery plaque

Aortic valve calcification and coronary artery calcium are different conditions in different structures. A calcium score from a CT scan measures artery calcium, not valve calcium. They can both be relevant — but they are not the same thing.

Calcification can progress slowly over years

Early valve calcification may cause no symptoms for a long time. An echocardiogram can detect calcification and assess valve function before significant narrowing develops — which is why it matters to identify risk early.

Family history matters here too

Because Lp(a) is largely genetically determined, the elevated valve calcification risk it carries can run in families. First-degree relatives of someone with elevated Lp(a) may share the same risk.

Not the same as a "heart murmur"

A heart murmur is a sound heard on examination that can have many causes. Aortic valve calcification can cause a murmur, but a murmur alone does not confirm calcification — proper imaging is needed for diagnosis.

No medication currently proven to stop progression

Unlike artery plaque, where statins reduce cardiovascular event risk, no drug has yet been shown to reliably slow the progression of established aortic valve calcification. This makes early identification and monitoring important.

Valve and artery risk should be discussed together

If your Lp(a) is elevated, ask your health team whether your risk picture should include both artery-focused assessment and valve-focused imaging. Both pathways matter when Lp(a) is part of your risk context.

Nine things to know about aortic stenosis

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above — crawlable, shareable, and worth reading before your next health team conversation.

What aortic stenosis is

Aortic stenosis is the narrowing of the aortic valve opening. As the valve becomes stiffer and more calcified, it cannot open as widely — restricting blood flow from the heart to the rest of the body.

How the heart responds

To push blood through a narrowed valve, the left ventricle must work harder. Over time, this increased pressure load can cause the heart muscle to thicken — a process called left ventricular hypertrophy.

The classic symptoms

Significant aortic stenosis can cause breathlessness on exertion, chest pain or tightness during activity, and fainting or near-fainting — particularly with physical effort. These symptoms require prompt medical assessment.

Symptoms can appear late

Aortic stenosis can be severe before symptoms develop. An echocardiogram can identify significant valve narrowing before breathlessness, chest pain, or fainting occurs — which is why monitoring matters in people at elevated risk.

Treatment options exist

Severe symptomatic aortic stenosis can be treated with valve replacement — either surgical (SAVR) or via a catheter procedure (TAVI/TAVR). The right approach depends on individual clinical factors and specialist assessment.

Lp(a) links to both valve and artery risk

If your Lp(a) is elevated, your risk picture may include both aortic valve disease and coronary artery disease. These pathways share a root cause — Lp(a) — but need separate assessment and management conversations.

Aortic stenosis is not just an elderly condition

While it becomes more common with age, elevated Lp(a) can accelerate valve calcification and stenosis. People with significantly elevated Lp(a) may develop meaningful valve disease earlier than population averages suggest.

No drug currently proven to prevent progression

Statins were studied for aortic stenosis progression and did not show benefit in large trials. Current management focuses on monitoring, identifying the right time for intervention, and managing coexisting cardiovascular risks.

This module is not a diagnosis or a timeline

This atlas shows an educational progression. It does not describe what is happening in any individual's heart or predict how quickly — or whether — disease will progress. Echocardiography and specialist assessment provide that picture.

Nine things to know about Lp(a) — stealth cholesterol

A plain-language summary of the main learning points from the visual guide above — crawlable, shareable, and worth reading before your next health team conversation.

What Lp(a) is

Lipoprotein(a) — written Lp(a) and pronounced "L-P-little-a" — is a cholesterol-carrying particle in the blood. It is structurally similar to LDL but has an additional protein called apolipoprotein(a) attached to it.

Why it is called stealth cholesterol

Lp(a) is not included in a standard cholesterol panel. Most people who have elevated Lp(a) have never been tested for it — making it genuinely hidden risk, present and active without any visible sign in routine results.

It is almost entirely genetic

Lp(a) levels are largely determined by the LPA gene and are mostly fixed from birth. Diet, exercise, and lifestyle have very little effect on Lp(a) levels — which is why testing and knowing your number matters.

It amplifies risk across two pathways

Elevated Lp(a) contributes to both artery disease — plaque, inflammation, rupture, clot, heart attack, stroke — and valve disease — aortic valve calcification and aortic stenosis. Most cardiovascular risk factors affect only one of these pathways.

How common it is

Roughly one in five people are estimated to have elevated Lp(a). Most have never been tested. Current guidelines from major cardiovascular organisations recommend measuring Lp(a) at least once in adulthood.

Family members may share the risk

Because Lp(a) is genetic, it runs in families. If your Lp(a) is elevated, your parents, siblings, and children have a meaningful probability of sharing that elevation. Testing can open important family conversations.

Lifestyle changes will not lower it significantly

Unlike LDL-C, Lp(a) does not respond meaningfully to diet, exercise, or standard statin therapy. This is one reason it is described as a distinct and underappreciated risk factor — and why knowing your level matters regardless of how healthy your lifestyle is.

High Lp(a) does not mean a heart attack is inevitable

Elevated Lp(a) increases risk — it does not guarantee an event. Risk management involves reducing other modifiable factors (LDL-C, ApoB, blood pressure, metabolic risk) that Lp(a) can amplify, and staying in active monitoring with your health team.

Specific treatments are in development

Several Lp(a)-targeting therapies are in clinical trials, including gene-silencing approaches. None are yet in routine clinical use, but the landscape is changing. Ask your health team about the current state of Lp(a)-specific treatment options.
