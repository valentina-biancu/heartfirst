Cholesterol Testing Is Foundational, Not Complete

A one-page summary of why the standard lipid panel remains essential, but is best understood as the first layer of cardiovascular risk assessment.

At a Glance
One of prevention's major successes

Routine cholesterol testing enabled the statin era and remains central to cardiovascular prevention worldwide.

LDL-C lowering is outcome-proven

Randomised trials show that each 1 mmol/L LDL-C reduction lowers major vascular events by approximately 22%.

"Routine" is not globally routine

In low- and middle-income countries, only about 40% of adults meeting WHO testing criteria have ever had cholesterol measured.

A "normal" LDL-C may not be the full picture

ApoB discordance, elevated Lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), metabolic risk, inflammatory risk, family history, or subclinical plaque may change interpretation.

"Baseline" is more accurate

The standard lipid panel is best understood as a foundational baseline assessment, not a complete cardiovascular risk evaluation.

Layered assessment is part of the future

Modern prevention should combine baseline lipids with risk context and selective escalation when evidence, risk, and resources justify it.

Lp(a) once, ApoB selectively

Guidelines increasingly support once-in-adulthood Lp(a) measurement, while ApoB can add precision in high-risk or discordant profiles.

Different settings need different priorities

Expand basic access where testing is absent; improve interpretation and layered assessment where baseline testing is already available.

The standard lipid panel should not be discarded. It should be understood as the first layer of cardiovascular risk assessment. Essential, proven, and actionable, but not always sufficient on its own.

What This Means in Practice

The standard panel still matters. The key is to use it well: as a baseline, in context, and with selective escalation when the story does not fit the panel alone.

Do not discard the standard panel

It remains scalable, familiar, inexpensive, and directly connected to proven treatment decisions.

Do not stop when risk seems discordant

If family history, metabolic risk, or clinical context do not match the panel, further assessment may be justified.

Repeat testing should have a purpose

Retesting is most useful when it informs treatment response, adherence, changing risk, or a specific decision.

Results should lead to action

Testing improves prevention only when results are interpreted, explained, and linked to clear next actions.

Use the standard lipid panel as a starting point, not a stopping point. Context matters. Follow-through matters. And in selected cases, a fuller risk picture matters too.
