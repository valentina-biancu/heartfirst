# The Quiet Epidemic: Heart Risks That Build Within the Body

**We are living inside a cardiovascular epidemic.**

**RECOGNISE · INTERNAL AND CARDIOMETABOLIC RISK**

Why heart risk can build quietly, how heart attack, stroke, and other cardiovascular damage can end or permanently change lives, and why prevention matters while there is still time to act.

> **THE QUESTION**
>
> What can place the heart and cardiovascular system under strain from within?

## At a glance

- Cardiovascular disease caused an estimated 19.8 million deaths in 2022 — close to one-third of all deaths worldwide. About 85% of those were heart attacks and strokes.[1]
- Death is not the only devastating outcome. Cardiovascular disease can cause permanent disability, loss of independence, financial hardship, and years of unpaid care for partners and families.
- Many heart risks develop silently. High blood pressure, high Lp(a), atherosclerosis, insulin resistance, and declining kidney function may cause no clear symptoms and can remain unknown until disease is advanced or a serious event occurs.
- Heart risk is not one thing. Blood pressure, glucose regulation, lipoproteins, kidney health, plaque, rhythm, valves, heart muscle, inflammation, clotting, and inherited factors all tell different stories.
- Prevention cannot guarantee every event will be avoided. But it creates opportunities to reduce risk, protect function, and act before a crisis removes choices.

> **Key takeaway**
>
> Prevention matters before the damage we are trying to prevent has happened. Recognising heart risks that may be building within the body can help protect life, function, independence, and the time still available to act.

## Why prevention is urgent

Time changes what is possible.

Most people do not wake up thinking they are living inside a cardiovascular epidemic.

They think heart attacks happen to other people. They think stroke is mainly an old-age problem. They think serious heart disease usually gives warning before it becomes dangerous.

Much of the time, that is not how this works.

Before a first heart attack, stroke, or heart-failure admission, prevention can:

- detect and manage persistent high blood pressure
- recognise impaired glucose regulation or diabetes
- reduce exposure to atherogenic particles
- uncover high Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), which is not included in a standard lipid panel
- identify kidney disease
- record a vital family history
- connect scattered results and diagnoses
- clarify what is known, what remains unknown, and what needs health-team attention.

After a diagnosis or event, prevention remains essential, but its purpose shifts: now it means preventing another event, slowing progression, protecting remaining heart and brain function, avoiding hospital admissions, and preserving independence.

The World Health Organization states that most cardiovascular diseases can be prevented by addressing behavioural and environmental risk factors, and by early detection and management of high blood pressure, diabetes, and high blood lipids.[1]

Prevention is not equally available to everyone. Some risks are inherited. Some damage has already occurred. Some exposures cannot be changed by individual choice alone. Access to testing, medicines, rehabilitation, safe environments, and time is unequal.

These limits matter. They do not make prevention meaningless.

> **Prevention is not certainty. It is the chance to act while there is still something meaningful to protect.**

That may mean protecting life. It may also mean protecting speech, movement, memory, independence, work, relationships, family stability, and years lived without avoidable cardiovascular damage.

## Some health problems announce themselves clearly. Heart risk often does not.

A broken bone hurts. An infection typically causes fever or pain. A visible rash alerts us to a skin problem.

Cardiovascular risk frequently offers no such warning.

High blood pressure may not ache. High Lp(a) usually causes no symptoms. Atherogenic particles can circulate for years without being felt. Plaque may develop in an artery wall long before it restricts blood flow enough to cause angina or breathlessness. Insulin resistance may be progressing while glucose readings still appear reassuring. Kidney function can decline quietly. An intermittent rhythm problem may come and go. A valve can narrow progressively without the person understanding why ordinary activity has become harder.

The first unmistakable sign can be a heart attack, stroke, collapse, or heart-failure admission. The World Health Organization notes that underlying blood-vessel disease may produce no symptoms, and that a heart attack or stroke may be the first sign.[1]

This does not mean every quiet process becomes a crisis. It means that feeling well cannot answer every question about cardiovascular health.

> **The event is visible. The process that led to it often is not.**

## The human toll: death, disability, and what follows survival

Cardiovascular disease is the world's leading cause of death. The 19.8 million lives lost in 2022 represent individual people — a parent, a spouse, a child, a sibling — each leaving behind a family and a future that vanished.

But death is not the only loss.

Many people survive a heart attack, stroke, or heart-failure episode. Some recover well. Others survive with damage that permanently alters their life.

Stroke may affect movement, speech, swallowing, memory, judgment, vision, mood, and the capacity to work or live independently. In 2021, stroke was the third leading cause of death and disability globally, with an estimated 93.8 million people living with its effects and 160 million disability-adjusted life years lost.[2]

A heart attack may leave damaged heart muscle. Heart failure can bring breathlessness, exhaustion, reduced mobility, repeated hospital stays, and growing dependence. Kidney damage may lead to complex treatment, dialysis, and further cardiovascular risk.

The person who once supported a household may become unable to work or may need help with walking, washing, dressing, eating, speaking, or making decisions.

A spouse or partner may become an unpaid carer, sometimes for life. Adult children may reduce work, interrupt education, or reorganise their families around care. Household income may fall while medical and care costs rise.

WHO recognises that carers of people affected by acute coronary syndrome and stroke can experience strain, financial burden, social isolation, and bereavement, and that cardiovascular disease can deepen poverty through treatment costs, lost income, and long-term care needs.[3]

> **The event may happen in minutes. Its effects can reshape a person and family for years or for life.**

This is the reality that makes prevention urgent. Not as a distant theoretical goal, but as the chance to avoid outcomes that cannot later be undone.

## One distinction that can change the conversation

People often use the words *risk factor*, *measurement*, *diagnosis*, and *established disease* as though they mean the same thing. They do not.

- **A risk factor** (e.g., high Lp(a), smoking, family history) increases the chance of disease or an event.
- **A measurement** (e.g., a single blood-pressure reading, an LDL-C value) records something at a moment in time. Its meaning depends on context, timing, and repetition.
- **A diagnosis** (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, familial hypercholesterolaemia) identifies a clinical condition based on defined criteria.
- **Established disease** (e.g., plaque on imaging, a previous heart attack, documented peripheral arterial disease) shows that part of the damaging process has already occurred.

For example:

- high Lp(a) is a risk factor;
- an Lp(a) result is a measurement;
- familial hypercholesterolaemia is a diagnosis;
- plaque found on imaging or a previous heart attack is evidence of established disease.

These categories can overlap, but treating them as interchangeable leads to confusion. A reassuring measurement cannot erase a diagnosis elsewhere. A risk factor does not predict an individual future with certainty. And a diagnosis does not tell us everything about severity or what will happen next.

Keeping these distinctions clear helps you ask better questions and avoid being falsely reassured by numbers that weren't designed to answer the question you're really asking.

## Heart risk is not one thing

The body does not organise heart risk into one number, one symptom, or one laboratory panel.

A person may think they have:

- a blood pressure problem
- a kidney problem
- a blood sugar problem
- a weight problem
- a cholesterol problem

In reality, they may be looking at several connected or coexisting risk processes appearing across different systems.

That is why one-number thinking fails. It is also why cardiovascular prevention cannot be reduced to one lab result, one symptom, or one specialist lens.

Heart risks that build within the body may involve:

- pressure and vascular load
- glucose regulation and insulin resistance
- lipids and lipoprotein particles
- kidney function
- plaque and established vascular disease
- heart rhythm and electrical conduction
- valves and heart structure
- heart muscle and pumping function
- inflammatory disease or activity
- clotting conditions
- inherited biology and family history.

Some share pathways. Some commonly occur together. Some add to total risk. Some change the importance of another finding. Some show that disease is already present.

The purpose of separating them is not academic — it is to prevent important information from being misunderstood, minimised, or lost.

## The landscape of internal risk

### Pressure and vascular load

Blood pressure is the force of blood against artery walls. It changes naturally throughout the day. When it remains high, the heart, arteries, brain, eyes, and kidneys are repeatedly exposed to additional load.

Persistent high blood pressure can:

- damage blood vessels
- contribute to plaque development
- increase the heart's workload
- thicken or weaken heart muscle
- damage the kidneys
- raise the risk of heart attack, stroke, heart failure, and other cardiovascular disease.[4]

The person may feel none of this. The artery wall experiences the pressure even when the person does not. The first obvious sign could be a stroke, heart attack, or kidney complication.

One blood pressure reading is still only one measurement. Repeated readings, how and when they were taken, medicines, illness, pain, stress, and the wider clinical context all affect interpretation.

> **Pressure becomes dangerous through repetition, duration, and the organs exposed to it.**

That is why identifying and managing persistent high blood pressure before damage occurs is one of prevention's clearest opportunities.

### Glucose regulation and metabolic strain

Insulin helps move glucose from the blood into cells. Insulin resistance develops when cells don't respond effectively, forcing the body to produce more insulin. Blood glucose may remain normal for a while, even as the metabolic system strains.

Over time, insulin resistance can be linked to:

- rising glucose or HbA1c
- type 2 diabetes
- higher triglycerides
- lower HDL cholesterol
- changes in atherogenic particle patterns
- higher blood pressure
- fat accumulation in and around organs
- metabolic liver disease
- inflammation and vascular dysfunction.[5]

Metabolic syndrome is a cluster of these conditions that raises cardiovascular risk. It is not one disease, and a person can have insulin resistance without every feature.

The consequences extend far beyond lab results. Diabetes and metabolic dysfunction can contribute to heart attack, stroke, kidney disease, nerve damage, vision loss, impaired healing, and years of treatment — all of which can affect independence, employment, and financial security. Recognising metabolic strain early is preventive cardiovascular work, not a judgement about body size or character.

> **Important distinction**
>
> Glucose, insulin, HbA1c, triglycerides, waist measurement, body weight, liver fat, and blood pressure provide different information. No one of them can describe the whole metabolic state. Body size alone does not diagnose metabolic health, and cardiometabolic risk can exist across a range of body sizes.

### Kidney health is heart health

The kidneys help regulate fluid, sodium, blood pressure, acid balance, and several hormonal systems. The heart and kidneys are closely connected. Cardiovascular disease can reduce kidney blood flow, and kidney disease increases cardiovascular risk through effects on blood pressure, fluid balance, blood vessels, mineral metabolism, inflammation, and anaemia.[6]

Kidney information may include:

- creatinine
- estimated glomerular filtration rate, or eGFR
- urine albumin
- a chronic kidney disease diagnosis
- change over time.

These values and diagnoses do not mean the same thing. A single eGFR result, for example, must be understood in context and may not by itself establish chronic kidney disease.

Kidney disease may remain quiet until function is substantially reduced. Advanced disease can bring severe fatigue, fluid problems, restricted daily life, dialysis, and greater dependence on family and health services.

Recognising kidney risk earlier can create opportunities to slow progression and reduce cardiovascular damage.

> **The heart can affect the kidneys. The kidneys can affect the heart. Neither should be considered in isolation.**

### Lipids, particles, and inherited risk

Cholesterol and triglycerides travel in blood inside lipoprotein particles. Atherogenic particles can enter the artery wall and contribute to atherosclerosis over time, often without symptoms. This exposure may continue for years. The first visible sign may be a heart attack, stroke, or plaque found during an investigation.

Different measurements answer different questions.

#### LDL cholesterol

LDL cholesterol, or LDL-C, estimates the amount of cholesterol carried inside low-density lipoprotein particles. Lowering LDL-C in appropriately selected people reduces atherosclerotic cardiovascular events.[7] But LDL-C measures cholesterol mass, not the number of circulating atherogenic particles.

#### ApoB

Apolipoprotein B, or ApoB, is found on the major atherogenic particles that can enter the artery wall. Because each major atherogenic particle generally carries one ApoB molecule, an ApoB test can provide information about particle number.

LDL-C and ApoB often move in the same direction, but they can disagree — particularly with insulin resistance, diabetes, higher triglycerides, kidney disease, or other cardiometabolic conditions.[7]

#### Triglycerides and triglyceride-rich particles

Triglycerides are another form of fat carried in blood. Higher triglycerides may form part of a wider metabolic pattern involving triglyceride-rich particles, remnant particles, glucose regulation, alcohol exposure, liver health, medicines, genetics, and other factors. A triglyceride result is useful information, but it cannot explain the entire metabolic state by itself.

#### Lipoprotein(a)

Almost everyone has some Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), but levels vary enormously and are largely genetically determined. High Lp(a) can independently increase cardiovascular risk. It is also associated with calcific aortic valve disease.[8]

Lp(a) is not included in a standard lipid panel. To find out your Lp(a) level, you must order an Lp(a) test.

Because high Lp(a) is inherited and usually causes no symptoms, families may discover it only after a premature heart attack, stroke, aortic-valve diagnosis, or the discovery of high Lp(a) in another relative.

That is the kind of delay HeartFirst exists to reduce.

The inherited level itself may not be something a person can choose or remove. Prevention still matters because blood pressure, smoking, ApoB-containing particles, diabetes, kidney disease, and established plaque can affect the wider cardiovascular context. What can be recognised, clarified, and managed around an inherited risk may still affect the likelihood and consequences of disease.

> **Go deeper: low Lp(a) and metabolic context**
>
> A low measured Lp(a) level is not an all-clear for overall cardiovascular or metabolic risk. Emerging evidence suggests that insulin resistance and higher insulin exposure may suppress apo(a) production and lower circulating Lp(a), so a low result may still need to be understood alongside glucose regulation and wider cardiometabolic information.[9] This is an evolving area. A low Lp(a) result does not diagnose insulin resistance, and the laboratory result should not automatically be described as inaccurate because metabolic risk is present.
>

### Plaque and established vascular disease

Atherosclerosis is the build-up of plaque in arteries. Risk factors and blood measurements can help estimate exposure or probability. They do not directly show whether plaque is present.

A previous heart attack, stroke, transient ischaemic attack, peripheral arterial disease, coronary procedure, or documented plaque is evidence of established disease — not merely risk. This changes the urgency and goals of prevention, which now include preventing recurrence, slowing progression, and protecting remaining function.

> **Risk asks what may happen. Established disease shows that part of the process has already occurred.**

A person with known atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease is not in the same position as someone with similar laboratory values but no known disease. This does not mean imaging is required for everyone or that one scan provides a complete cardiovascular verdict. It means that established disease must not be minimised because another result appears reassuring.

### Rhythm, valves, heart muscle, and structure

Not all heart disease begins with plaque.

The heart can also be affected by:

- abnormal rhythms or electrical-conduction problems
- valve narrowing or leakage
- weakened, thickened, stiff, inflamed, scarred, or infiltrated heart muscle
- congenital heart conditions
- disease of the aorta
- inherited cardiomyopathies or rhythm conditions.

Some of these conditions are inherited. Some are acquired. Some develop with age, infection, pressure load, metabolic disease, pregnancy, medicines, or other processes.

They may coexist with cardiometabolic risk, but they must not be absorbed into a generic cardiometabolic category. A reassuring cholesterol or blood-pressure result cannot evaluate every question about rhythm, valves, structure, or heart muscle.

Some rhythm problems, particularly atrial fibrillation, can increase stroke risk. A severely narrowed valve may lead to fainting, heart failure, and loss of capacity. Heart-muscle disease can cause breathlessness, fatigue, dangerous rhythms, hospital admissions, and sudden death.

Recognising these conditions before severe damage or crisis may affect surveillance, treatment, family assessment, and the protection of function.

### Inflammation and clotting remain distinct

Inflammation and clotting are both essential biological systems. Inflammation helps the body respond to injury and infection. Clotting helps prevent uncontrolled bleeding.

Both may become relevant to cardiovascular disease in particular circumstances, but they are not the same process. Inflammatory activity is involved in atherosclerosis and in the body's response to tissue injury. Some inflammatory diseases are associated with increased cardiovascular risk, and some inflammatory markers may add information in selected clinical settings. But one inflammatory marker cannot establish whether plaque is present, predict whether an event will occur, or determine what treatment a person needs.

Clotting becomes dangerous when a clot forms or persists in the wrong place. A clot may block blood flow in an artery or vein. It may be relevant to heart attack, ischaemic stroke, pulmonary embolism, atrial fibrillation, particular inherited or acquired clotting disorders, medicines, cancer, surgery, immobility, pregnancy, and other specific circumstances.

A clot-related event may kill quickly. It may also leave permanent brain, heart, lung, limb, or organ damage. That seriousness is why clotting must be described precisely rather than reduced to vague language such as "thick blood."

> **Important distinction**
>
> Inflammation, clotting, plaque, and blood-vessel injury may meet in some cardiovascular events, but they must not be collapsed into one generic risk category.

## Inherited and family risk

Some internal risks begin with biology we inherit. Examples include:

- high Lp(a)
- familial hypercholesterolaemia
- inherited cardiomyopathies
- inherited rhythm conditions
- some aortic and connective-tissue disorders
- some valve conditions
- inherited tendencies affecting blood pressure, metabolism, or clotting.

Family history can provide an early clue that inherited or shared risk may deserve attention. Relevant information may include:

- heart attack or stroke at a young age
- sudden or unexplained death
- very high cholesterol
- known high Lp(a)
- cardiomyopathy
- arrhythmia
- heart failure
- aortic disease
- valve disease
- diabetes or kidney disease across several relatives.

Family history is not destiny. But it should not be dismissed as a vague family story when it may be the only visible sign of a risk that has not yet been tested or diagnosed.

When one premature event leads other family members to clarify their own risk and act sooner, that loss may help change what happens next.

## Connected does not mean interchangeable

The body is connected.

High blood pressure can affect the kidneys, brain, arteries, and heart. Kidney disease can change blood pressure, fluid balance, medicine choices, and cardiovascular risk. Insulin resistance can affect glucose, triglycerides, blood pressure, liver health, and particle patterns. High ApoB or high Lp(a) can increase atherosclerotic risk. Established plaque can change the importance and urgency of other information. An arrhythmia, valve condition, or cardiomyopathy may alter the wider context even when cardiometabolic results appear reassuring.

But connection does not mean that every risk multiplies every other risk or that every combination has a proven biological interaction.

A safer way to think is:

- some factors share pathways
- some commonly coexist
- some add to total risk
- some modify the meaning of another finding
- some must remain separate
- some relationships are unresolved and require professional interpretation.

This is why one-number thinking fails. It is also why scattered information can be dangerous. A result that looks reassuring in isolation may have different meaning beside a diagnosis, family history, symptom, medicine, or evidence of established disease.

## Prevention still has leverage

The scale of cardiovascular death and disability is enormous. The process can be silent. The first visible sign can be a major event.

None of this makes prevention hopeless.

Many important risks can be recognised, measured, monitored, discussed, treated, reduced, and placed in context before a crisis.

Not every risk can be removed. Not every person has equal access. But preventing one heart attack, one disabling stroke, one progression to kidney failure matters completely to the person and family who do not have to live through it.

> **Population statistics are made of individual lives. Prevention changes the numbers by changing what happens to people.**

This is not an invitation to panic or chase every test. It is a reason to take incomplete information seriously — before an event makes the missing pieces impossible to ignore.

## What this guide does not claim

Recognising the urgency of cardiovascular prevention does not mean:

- that everyone has hidden cardiovascular disease or is in immediate danger
- that every risk factor will lead to heart attack, stroke, disability, or death
- that every event could have been predicted or prevented
- that one abnormal or reassuring result explains the whole situation
- that all risks interact or amplify one another in the same way
- that everyone needs every available test, scan, genetic test, or specialist review
- that more testing is automatically better
- that a person is to blame for inherited biology, illness, disability, body composition, environmental exposure, or barriers to care
- that this guide can determine personal tests, diagnoses, treatment, medicines, diet, or exercise.

Personal interpretation belongs with an appropriately qualified health professional who can listen to the person, consider symptoms and concerns, review history and family information, examine relevant measurements and records, explain what they may mean, discuss the available options and their consequences, and decide with the person what action is appropriate.

## What this means for you

You do not need to carry the global burden in your head. But recognising the landscape you're standing in matters.

Important information may already exist in different places:

- a laboratory report
- a blood-pressure record
- a diagnosis
- an imaging result
- a hospital or specialist letter
- a family history
- a medicine list
- a result without a date or unit
- a question that has never been answered
- a test assumed to be part of routine care but never ordered.

You may not need more information everywhere. You may need the information you already have to be gathered, checked, connected, and explained.

A useful next step may be to:

- separate what is known from what is unknown — and what has been assumed
- distinguish a risk factor from a measurement, a diagnosis, or established disease
- notice which heart-risk areas have been assessed and which remain unknown
- stop treating one reassuring result as a verdict on the whole cardiovascular system
- organise dates, units, sources, and changes over time
- recognise where delay may allow risk or damage to continue
- prepare focused questions
- agree one appropriate next action with your health team.

When a problem is common, quiet, layered, and consequential, organising information is not paperwork for its own sake.

> **Structure becomes part of prevention when it helps turn scattered information into something that can be listened to, understood, discussed, and acted upon.**

## Questions worth considering

- Which heart-risk areas are already known: pressure, glucose regulation, lipids, kidney health, plaque, rhythm, valves, heart muscle, inherited factors, inflammation, or clotting?
- Which pieces of information are measurements, which are diagnoses, and which show established disease or damage?
- Do I have the original results, dates, units, reports, and relevant medicine information?
- Is there a test I assumed was included in routine care but that was never ordered?
- Am I relying on one reassuring result to answer parts of heart risk it was not designed to assess?
- Has anything changed over time, or am I comparing results taken under different conditions?
- Is important family information recorded accurately, including who was affected and at what age?
- Is there a result, diagnosis, symptom, or family event I have been trying to interpret without enough context?
- Which consequence am I most trying to help prevent: a first event, a repeat event, progression, disability, loss of independence, or further damage?
- What do I most need my health team to listen to, explain, or clarify?
- What is one appropriate next action: obtain a record, organise existing information, continue learning, or prepare a health team conversation?

These questions do not determine which risks you have or what care you need. They help separate what is known from what is assumed, and what can still be done from what has been left unexamined.
