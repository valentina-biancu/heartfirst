# The Risks We Live With

**The conditions around us can become conditions within us.**

**RECOGNISE · LIFESTYLE AND ENVIRONMENT**

How the air we breathe, the food available to us, movement, sleep, tobacco and nicotine, alcohol, stress, work, relationships, and the conditions around us can shape heart risk — and why prevention cannot be reduced to personal choice.

> **THE QUESTION**
>
> What in our lives and surroundings can shape cardiovascular strain?

## At a glance

- Cardiovascular risk is shaped not only by what happens inside the body, but also by what we breathe, eat, drink, do, endure, and can access.
- Tobacco, second-hand smoke, unhealthy dietary patterns, physical inactivity, harmful alcohol use, and air pollution are established cardiovascular risks.[1–6]
- Sleep, chronic stress, long working hours, social isolation, noise, heat, and wider social conditions can also affect cardiovascular health or the ability to protect it.[7–14]
- Some exposures are chosen. Many are constrained, imposed by work or place, shaped by circumstances, or difficult to change without support.
- “Modifiable” does not mean easy, immediate, equally changeable, or a matter of individual responsibility alone.
- Prevention may involve personal action, health-team support, family and community change, safer workplaces, public policy, and access to appropriate care.

> **Key takeaway**
>
> The cardiovascular system responds to exposure, not to whether the exposure was chosen. Effective prevention recognises both personal agency and the conditions that make healthy action possible, difficult, or impossible.

## Heart risk is lived, not only measured

The cardiovascular epidemic is not built only from processes inside the body.

It is also shaped by the lives people are able to live.

It can be shaped by:

- smoke at home or work;
- food that is affordable, available, marketed, and culturally familiar;
- whether movement is safe, accessible, and physically possible;
- night shifts, long hours, interrupted sleep, pain, or caring responsibilities;
- alcohol used socially, habitually, or to manage distress;
- polluted air, traffic noise, heat, and poor housing;
- isolation, insecurity, discrimination, and chronic stress;
- whether a person can reach care, pay for it, understand it, and act on it.

These conditions do not remain outside us.

They can influence blood pressure, glucose regulation, lipid patterns, inflammation, vascular function, sleep, heart rhythm, kidney strain, body composition, and the development or progression of cardiovascular disease.[1,2,8,9]

This does not mean every exposure causes disease in every person.

It means that cardiovascular risk is partly biological and partly lived.

Throughout this guide, **heart risk** is used as plain-language shorthand for risk affecting the heart and the wider cardiovascular system, including the blood vessels, brain, and kidneys.

> **The body carries the consequences of the conditions in which a person lives.**

## “Lifestyle” is an incomplete word

The word *lifestyle* is often used as though it describes a set of freely chosen habits.

Sometimes it does.

A person may have some control over smoking, drinking, food, movement, sitting, or sleep.

But control is rarely complete, and choice is rarely the whole explanation.

A person may smoke because nicotine is addictive and smoking became a way to manage fear, trauma, stress, hunger, or social pressure.

A person may buy cheaper, heavily processed food because fresh food is unaffordable, unavailable, takes time they do not have, or cannot be stored or prepared where they live.

A person may spend most of the day sitting because of disability, pain, unsafe streets, shift work, transport, caring responsibilities, or a job designed around prolonged sitting.

A person may sleep badly because of night work, a crying baby, menopause, pain, anxiety, sleep apnoea, noise, overcrowding, or caring for someone who cannot be left alone.

A person may breathe polluted air because that is where their home, school, or job is located.

The phrase *lifestyle risk* becomes harmful when it turns conditions into character.

This guide uses **lifestyle and environment** together because they overlap.

What people do matters.

So do the conditions in which they are expected to do it.

## Modifiable does not mean easy

Many cardiovascular resources describe tobacco use, diet, activity, alcohol, sleep, or stress as *modifiable risk factors*.

The term has value. It means that risk is not necessarily fixed and that change may be possible.

But it is often misunderstood.

**Modifiable does not mean:**

- entirely chosen;
- easy to change;
- equally changeable for everyone;
- changeable without money, time, safety, treatment, or support;
- under the control of one person alone;
- proof that a person caused their illness;
- proof that an event would have been prevented if they had “tried harder”.

A risk may be modifiable in principle while being extremely difficult to modify in practice.

A neighbourhood may be walkable in theory but unsafe after dark.

A person may know that smoking is harmful but still be living with nicotine dependence.

A family may understand healthy eating while facing food insecurity.

A shift worker may know that sleep matters and still be unable to keep regular hours.

A person with heart failure, arthritis, breathlessness, disability, or severe fatigue may need adapted activity and clinical guidance, not an instruction to exercise more.

> **Prevention becomes more humane and more effective when it asks not only “What should change?” but also “What would make change possible?”**

## How the outside can become the inside

Lifestyle and environmental exposures do not form a separate cardiovascular system.

They can act through the internal processes described in **The Quiet Epidemic: Heart Risks That Build Within the Body**.

For example:

- tobacco smoke can injure blood vessels, promote atherosclerosis, affect clotting, and increase heart attack and stroke risk;
- high-salt dietary patterns can contribute to high blood pressure;
- insufficient activity can affect blood pressure, insulin sensitivity, glucose, lipids, fitness, and function;
- repeated sleep disruption can affect blood pressure, glucose regulation, appetite, stress responses, and recovery;
- harmful alcohol use can affect blood pressure, heart rhythm, heart muscle, liver health, and stroke risk;
- air pollution can contribute to heart disease and stroke;
- heat can place additional strain on the heart and kidneys;
- chronic stress can affect sleep, blood pressure, behaviour, and the ability to sustain care;
- poverty, unsafe housing, insecure work, and poor access to care can increase exposure while reducing the options available to respond.

The pathway is not always simple.

One exposure may act through several systems.

Several exposures may cluster around the same person.

The same exposure may have different effects depending on age, inherited risk, existing disease, medicines, pregnancy, disability, occupation, and access to support.

This is why external risk cannot be reduced to a checklist of “good” and “bad” behaviour.

The sections that follow look at major exposures one by one. In real life, they rarely arrive alone. Their effects can depend on duration, intensity, timing, underlying health, access to recovery and protection, and the person they meet.

## Tobacco, nicotine, and smoke that belongs to someone else

Tobacco is one of the clearest preventable causes of cardiovascular disease.

Smoking damages blood vessels and increases the risk of coronary heart disease, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, and premature death. Tobacco harms nearly every organ, and there is no safe level of exposure to second-hand tobacco smoke.[3]

Second-hand smoke matters because cardiovascular exposure is not limited to the person smoking.

A partner, child, colleague, hospitality worker, neighbour, or passenger may be exposed without choosing it.

Nicotine is addictive. This matters because an addictive exposure cannot be understood as a simple failure of will.

Different tobacco and nicotine products do not carry identical risks. Combustible tobacco is especially harmful, but newer products should not be assumed harmless or heart-safe.

For a person who uses tobacco or nicotine, prevention may involve:

- being asked without judgement;
- understanding what is being used and how often;
- recognising household and workplace exposure;
- support for dependence rather than blame;
- evidence-based cessation treatment;
- repeated attempts rather than one all-or-nothing test of character;
- protecting other people from smoke and aerosol exposure.

Stopping tobacco use can reduce cardiovascular risk, but the route to stopping may require professional help, medicines, behavioural support, family cooperation, and changes in the environment.

> **The right question is not only “Why haven’t you stopped?” It is also “What is keeping this exposure in place, and what support would make stopping more possible?”**

## Food, nutrition, and the environment in which people eat

Food matters to cardiovascular health.

Dietary patterns can influence blood pressure, glucose regulation, blood lipids, body composition, kidney health, and the development of cardiovascular disease.

WHO guidance supports dietary patterns that include a variety of minimally processed foods, vegetables and fruit, whole grains, pulses, nuts, and appropriate sources of protein, while limiting excess sodium, free sugars, saturated fat, and industrial trans fats.[4]

But a heart-supportive diet is not one perfect menu.

It is not one forbidden ingredient.

It is not a moral ranking of people according to what is on their plate.

Food is also:

- culture;
- family;
- pleasure;
- comfort;
- celebration;
- work;
- money;
- access;
- time;
- storage;
- cooking facilities;
- health conditions;
- appetite;
- medication effects;
- caregiving;
- commercial influence.

The food most heavily promoted, cheapest, closest, quickest, or easiest to store may not be the food a health professional would recommend.

Advice that ignores this reality may be technically correct and practically useless.

A better prevention conversation asks:

- What does the person normally eat?
- What food is available and affordable?
- Are there cultural or religious patterns to respect?
- Is salt coming mainly from cooking, packaged foods, restaurant food, or all three?
- Are there kidney, metabolic, gastrointestinal, swallowing, allergy, or medicine considerations?
- Who buys and prepares the food?
- What is one realistic improvement that can be sustained?

No single food causes or prevents cardiovascular disease by itself.

Patterns, quantity, frequency, substitution, and the wider health context matter.

> **Nutrition guidance becomes preventive when it can be lived, not merely prescribed.**

## Movement, fitness, disability, and the time spent sitting

Regular physical activity helps prevent and manage heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, diabetes, and other chronic conditions. WHO also advises limiting sedentary time and emphasises that any amount of physical activity is better than none.[5]

Movement can support:

- cardiovascular fitness;
- blood-pressure control;
- insulin sensitivity;
- glucose regulation;
- strength and balance;
- mobility and independence;
- sleep;
- mood;
- recovery and resilience.

But movement is not the same as sport.

It can include walking, wheeling, cycling, housework, occupational movement, active transport, rehabilitation, adapted exercise, dancing, gardening, play, and short periods of activity throughout the day.

Advice must also respect capacity and safety.

A person with chest symptoms, fainting, significant breathlessness, unstable disease, recent surgery, advanced heart failure, severe hypertension, disability, or another clinical concern may need assessment and tailored guidance.

A person with pain, fatigue, limited mobility, unsafe streets, no childcare, or exhausting work may need a different starting point from someone with time, money, safety, and full physical capacity.

Sedentary work can remain a risk even for someone who exercises at another time. At the same time, prolonged sitting may be unavoidable for a person with disability or illness.

The aim is not to punish sitting or glorify exercise.

It is to recognise where safe, appropriate movement can protect function and reduce cardiovascular strain.

> **No single form of exercise is required. The most useful movement is the movement a person can do safely, repeat, and build upon.**

## Sleep, recovery, and the hours people do not control

Sleep is part of cardiovascular health.

Most adults need roughly seven to nine hours, although individual needs and circumstances vary. Sleep duration is only one part of the picture. Timing, regularity, quality, interruptions, shift work, and sleep disorders also matter.[7]

Poor or disrupted sleep may affect:

- blood pressure;
- glucose regulation;
- appetite and eating patterns;
- stress responses;
- mood and decision-making;
- energy for movement;
- the ability to manage medicines and care.

Obstructive sleep apnoea is a clinical sleep disorder associated with increased cardiovascular risk. It is not simply “bad sleep” and cannot be solved by generic sleep-hygiene advice alone.[7,15]

Possible signs include breathing that stops and restarts during sleep, gasping for air, frequent loud snoring, and unexplained daytime sleepiness or fatigue. Because some signs happen during sleep, another person may notice them first. These signs vary, and their presence does not by itself establish a diagnosis.[7]

Sleep is also where personal-control language often fails.

A person cannot always choose uninterrupted rest.

They may be:

- working nights;
- caring for a baby, child, partner, or parent;
- living with pain, menopause symptoms, anxiety, trauma, or depression;
- sharing crowded or noisy housing;
- affected by heat;
- living with untreated sleep apnoea;
- working several jobs;
- unable to control work schedules.

Prevention may involve habits, but it may also require diagnosis, treatment, workplace change, noise reduction, family support, pain care, mental-health care, or safer housing.

> **Telling an exhausted person to sleep more is not a plan. Understanding why sleep is being lost may be.**

## Alcohol and the myth of a required “heart-healthy” drink

Alcohol is deeply embedded in many cultures and social lives.

It may also be used to relax, sleep, cope, celebrate, belong, or escape.

Alcohol consumption is associated with cardiovascular and other health harms. WHO attributed an estimated 474,000 cardiovascular deaths in 2019 to alcohol consumption.[6]

Cardiovascular effects can include:

- increased blood pressure;
- heart-rhythm disturbance;
- stroke;
- damage to heart muscle;
- effects on triglycerides, liver health, sleep, medicines, and judgement.

Risk varies with amount, pattern, frequency, binge drinking, individual biology, medicines, and existing conditions.

Alcohol should not be started or prescribed as a heart-protection strategy.

For some people, reducing intake may be appropriate.

For others, abstinence is the safer option.

A person with alcohol dependence may be at risk if they stop suddenly without medical support. This is another reason the conversation must move beyond instruction and towards assessment.

A useful discussion asks what alcohol is doing in the person’s life, not only how many units are being consumed.

> **A substance used to manage stress, loneliness, pain, or sleep cannot be addressed fully without addressing the reason it became useful.**

## Stress, work, care, and the demand that never switches off

Stress is not automatically harmful.

Short-term stress helps the body respond to danger, challenge, or change. The concern is repeated, severe, or persistent stress without adequate recovery, safety, control, or support.

A stressful situation activates the autonomic nervous system and stress hormones. Heart rate and blood pressure may rise. When this response is repeated or sustained, it may contribute to high blood pressure, inflammation, disrupted sleep, harmful coping patterns, and difficulty maintaining medicines, appointments, or other preventive care.[8,16]

The source matters.

Stress may come from:

- insecure or unsafe work;
- long hours, low control, or relentless demand;
- financial insecurity;
- caregiving without adequate help or respite;
- bereavement, trauma, discrimination, or violence;
- chronic illness or pain;
- fear of losing housing or legal status;
- loneliness;
- a health system that repeatedly fails to listen.

The evidence on working time shows why stress cannot always be treated as a private coping problem. WHO and the International Labour Organization estimated that working 55 hours or more each week contributed to 745,000 deaths from stroke and ischaemic heart disease in 2016. Compared with working 35–40 hours a week, this level of exposure was associated with an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and a 17% higher risk of dying from ischaemic heart disease.[14]

The answer cannot always be “manage stress better”.

> **A breathing exercise cannot repair an unsafe workplace, replace lost income, provide respite care, or end discrimination.**

Personal tools may still help. So may mental-health care, social connection, workplace protections, financial support, family help, and changes to the source of strain.

Stress prevention becomes credible when it addresses both the response inside the person and the pressure outside them.

## Social connection, loneliness, and the people around us

Relationships can protect health by providing belonging, practical help, encouragement, shared meals, transport, care, and someone who notices when something has changed.

Social isolation and loneliness can harm physical and mental health. WHO identifies links with heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, depression, anxiety, cognitive decline, and premature death.[10]

Loneliness is not the same as being alone.

A person may live alone and feel connected.

Another may live with family and feel profoundly isolated.

Cardiovascular prevention can be affected when a person has no one to:

- help them reach care;
- understand instructions;
- notice symptoms;
- collect medicines;
- share food or activity;
- support recovery;
- provide respite;
- encourage a difficult change.

Social connection is not a substitute for medical care, and loneliness is not a personal failure.

But relationships and support can change what prevention and recovery are possible.

## Air pollution: a cardiovascular exposure people cannot simply opt out of

Air pollution is not only a lung problem.

WHO identifies stroke and ischaemic heart disease among the major health outcomes linked to ambient air pollution. In 2019, an estimated 68% of premature deaths attributed to outdoor air pollution were due to ischaemic heart disease and stroke.[11]

Exposure may come from:

- road traffic;
- industry;
- power generation;
- fires and smoke;
- household fuels;
- occupational sources;
- neighbourhood design.

Individual steps may sometimes reduce exposure, but they have limits.

A person cannot solve regional air pollution by breathing differently.

They may not be able to move home, change jobs, buy filtration, avoid commuting, or keep children indoors.

This is why environmental prevention requires public action as well as personal advice:

- cleaner transport and energy;
- safer workplaces;
- air-quality standards;
- smoke-free environments;
- urban planning;
- public warnings and protections during severe pollution.

> **Some heart risks require a policy response because the exposure is shared and the individual cannot remove it alone.**

## Noise, heat, housing, and the built environment

The places where people live and work can affect sleep, stress, movement, exposure, and cardiovascular strain.

### Noise

Environmental noise can disturb sleep and is associated with hypertension and ischaemic heart disease. Traffic, aircraft, rail, industry, and crowded housing may create repeated exposure that a person cannot control.[12]

Noise is not merely an annoyance when it repeatedly disrupts rest and recovery.

### Heat

Heat places additional strain on the body as it tries to regulate temperature.

WHO notes that extreme heat can worsen cardiovascular and kidney conditions and contribute to acute kidney injury. Older adults, people with chronic conditions, people taking particular medicines, outdoor workers, and those without adequate cooling may be especially vulnerable.[13]

Heat protection may involve hydration and behaviour, but it also depends on housing, electricity, shade, work rules, urban design, early warnings, and access to care.

### The built environment

Neighbourhoods can make movement safer or harder.

They can provide:

- pavements;
- shade;
- parks;
- public transport;
- local food shops;
- health services;
- social spaces;
- protection from traffic and pollution.

Or they can remove them.

A person’s address can therefore shape exposure and opportunity without determining their future.

## Money, education, housing, and access to care

WHO defines the social determinants of health as the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, together with the wider forces shaping daily life.[9]

These conditions influence:

- what food is affordable;
- whether housing is safe and stable;
- whether work allows rest or appointments;
- whether movement is safe;
- exposure to pollution and heat;
- access to education and understandable health information;
- the ability to pay for testing, medicines, transport, rehabilitation, and care;
- whether a person is believed and treated with dignity.

Commercial environments also shape health.

Products are designed, priced, promoted, and placed in ways that influence tobacco, alcohol, food, and other exposures.[9]

This does not remove personal agency.

It explains why agency operates within conditions.

Two people can receive the same advice and face entirely different possibilities for acting on it.

> **The healthy choice is not equally available, affordable, safe, understandable, or supported.**

Prevention that ignores this may widen inequality by helping most those who already have the greatest resources.

## Risks can cluster around the same life

Lifestyle and environmental risks do not always arrive one at a time.

One person may be living with:

- shift work;
- poor sleep;
- financial strain;
- long sitting;
- cheap convenience food;
- tobacco dependence;
- polluted air;
- caring responsibilities;
- little time for appointments;
- no safe place to move;
- a family history that has never been recorded.

This does not mean every exposure biologically multiplies every other one.

It means that the same life can contain several pressures while offering very little room to respond.

A plan that addresses only one behaviour may fail because the surrounding conditions remain unchanged.

For example:

- sleep may not improve until shift patterns or sleep apnoea are addressed;
- activity may not increase until pain, safety, or childcare is addressed;
- tobacco cessation may be harder while severe stress remains untreated;
- dietary change may be unrealistic without affordability and preparation support;
- medicines may not be taken consistently when cost, side effects, literacy, or transport remain unresolved.

This is why listening matters.

The person living the risk environment may understand the barriers better than anyone looking at the results.

## Prevention happens at more than one level

Cardiovascular prevention is often presented as a list of instructions for individuals.

Individual action matters.

It is not the only level at which prevention happens.

### Personal

A person may be able to:

- seek help with tobacco or alcohol;
- add safe movement;
- reduce sedentary time;
- improve part of their dietary pattern;
- protect sleep where possible;
- record exposures and barriers;
- ask for assessment of symptoms or sleep problems;
- strengthen social connection;
- prepare a focused health-team conversation.

### Health team

A health team may be able to:

- listen before advising;
- assess capacity, symptoms, medicines, and conditions;
- treat dependence;
- diagnose sleep disorders;
- adapt activity;
- refer to dietetic, rehabilitation, psychological, social, or community support;
- consider affordability, disability, work, culture, and caregiving;
- agree realistic priorities rather than issue an impossible list.

### Family and community

Families and communities may be able to:

- create smoke-free spaces;
- share food preparation;
- support movement and appointments;
- provide care or respite;
- reduce isolation;
- make changes together rather than single out one person.

### Workplace and public policy

Systems may be able to:

- control air pollution and environmental noise;
- protect people from second-hand smoke;
- improve food environments;
- make streets safer;
- regulate working hours and occupational exposure;
- provide heat protection;
- support housing and transport;
- make prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation accessible.

> **When the risk is created or maintained beyond the individual, part of the solution must also exist beyond the individual.**

## What this guide does not claim

Recognising lifestyle and environmental cardiovascular risk does not mean:

- that every exposure will cause disease or affect every person in the same way;
- that everyone has equal control over risk, or that people are responsible for pollution, poverty, unsafe work, addiction, disability, trauma, or lack of access;
- that personal choices do not matter, or that one person can solve a structural problem alone;
- that changing one habit will erase inherited risk, established disease, or every other exposure;
- that body size alone defines health, effort, or cardiovascular risk;
- that all stress is harmful, or that everyone needs the same diet, movement, sleep, or alcohol plan;
- that supplements, extreme diets, or intensive exercise are required;
- that this guide can determine personal treatment or replace professional assessment.

Lifestyle and environmental prevention should be adapted to the person’s health, capacity, circumstances, priorities, culture, and access to support.

Personal interpretation belongs with appropriately qualified health professionals who can listen to the person, consider symptoms, diagnoses, medicines, dependence, disability, pregnancy, work, sleep, mental health, and access to care; assess when clinical evaluation or treatment is needed; explain the available options and their possible benefits and harms; and agree with the person what action is appropriate.

## What this means for you

> **The conditions around us can become conditions within us. But they do not all require the same kind of response.**

You do not need to correct every part of your life before prevention can begin.

You do not need to accept blame for conditions you did not choose.

And you do not need to pretend that personal action is powerless because some risks are structural.

A useful starting point is to ask:

- Which exposures are present?
- Which can I change directly?
- Which could change with support?
- Which need health-team attention?
- Which are controlled by my household, workplace, landlord, community, or government?
- Which one matters enough and is possible enough to address first?

You may discover that the most useful next action is not “try harder”.

It may be:

- asking for tobacco-dependence treatment;
- discussing alcohol honestly;
- investigating disrupted sleep;
- finding movement that fits your capacity;
- changing one repeated food pattern;
- documenting a work or environmental exposure;
- asking for a medicine or symptom review;
- involving family;
- seeking financial, social, rehabilitation, or mental-health support;
- naming the barrier that has made previous advice impossible to follow.

> **Prevention becomes real when advice meets the life in which it must be carried out.**

## Questions worth considering

### Questions about your own exposures

- Which parts of my daily life may be shaping cardiovascular strain?
- Am I exposed to tobacco smoke, nicotine, polluted air, excessive noise, heat, or occupational hazards?
- Is alcohol being used for pleasure, habit, sleep, stress, pain, belonging, or escape?
- Is my food pattern shaped mainly by preference, culture, cost, access, time, health needs, or commercial pressure?
- What forms of movement are safe and realistic for me?
- Is poor sleep a routine problem, a circumstance, or a possible clinical disorder?
- Do stress, loneliness, depression, anxiety, trauma, or isolation need to be part of my cardiovascular-health conversation?

### Questions about the conditions shaping them

- How are work, caregiving, pain, disability, housing, or money affecting sleep, food, movement, medicines, or appointments?
- Which risk have I been blamed for without being offered practical support?
- Which change is within my control now?
- Which change would require family, clinical, workplace, community, or policy support?
- What is one barrier my health team needs to understand before giving advice?
- What is one useful next action that I could realistically sustain?

These questions are not a score and do not diagnose cardiovascular risk.

They are a way to recognise the conditions shaping it.

This second draft retains the blank-canvas architecture.

The old external-risk material has not been used as its organising structure. It can be reviewed later as a recovery source for evidence, examples, explanations, or passages worth preserving.

Version 0.2 added a verified reading-time range, a clearer bridge into the exposure sections, Recognise-level signs of possible sleep apnoea, a more focused stress and long-working-hours section, compressed boundaries, grouped reflection questions, and a terminology clarification for **heart risk** and **cardiovascular risk**.

The draft is built around five principles:

1. lifestyle and environment are connected but not interchangeable;
2. personal action matters, but risk is not produced by personal choice alone;
3. “modifiable” must not be used as a synonym for easy or blameworthy;
4. external exposures often act through internal cardiovascular processes;
5. prevention may require action at personal, clinical, family, community, workplace, and policy levels.
