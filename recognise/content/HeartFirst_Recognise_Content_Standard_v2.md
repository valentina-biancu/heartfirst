# HeartFirst Recognise Content Standard

**Status:** Working editorial standard, version 2  
**Applies to:** HeartFirst Recognise resources  
**Journey:** Recognise → Clarify → Navigate → Prevent → Protect  
**Primary public location:** `heartfirst.shyntesy.com/recognise/`  
**Matching commercial path:** `shyntesy.com/recognise/`

---

## 1. Purpose of Recognise

Recognise is the free education and trust layer of HeartFirst.

Its job is to help people recognise:

- the cardiovascular system we are trying to protect;
- the risks and disease processes that can develop within the body;
- the lifestyle, environmental, occupational, and social conditions that may shape cardiovascular strain;
- the value and limits of the standard cholesterol test;
- what is already known, what may remain unknown or incomplete, and why clarification can matter.

Recognise does not assess an individual, interpret personal results, calculate risk, prescribe prevention, or provide continuing monitoring.

It creates a strong and honest foundation for the rest of the HeartFirst journey:

- **Clarify:** organise knowns, unknowns, discussion points, and next action;
- **Navigate:** examine context, relationships, distinctions, and unresolved questions;
- **Prevent:** carry an agreed action into recorded and reported results;
- **Protect:** support continuity across future prevention loops.

Recognise is open access. It is not a paid prerequisite and it should not imply that every reader must follow a fixed sequence.

## 2. Locked v1 Recognise spine

Recognise is built around four complementary foundations.

| Public title | Kicker | The question | Primary job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Heart We Are Trying to Protect** | RECOGNISE · THE HEART | **What are we trying to protect?** | Explain the heart and cardiovascular system, including mechanical, electrical, vascular, valve, blood, flow, pressure, and reserve dimensions. |
| **The Risks That Build Within the Body** | RECOGNISE · INTERNAL AND CARDIOMETABOLIC RISK | **What can place the cardiovascular system under strain from within?** | Explain internal risk processes while keeping cardiometabolic, inherited, kidney, lipid, plaque, rhythm, structural, inflammatory, clotting, and established-disease information distinct. |
| **The Risks We Live With** | RECOGNISE · LIFESTYLE AND ENVIRONMENT | **What in our lives and surroundings can shape cardiovascular strain?** | Explain lifestyle and environmental risk without blame, including unequal control over exposure and resources. |
| **Your Cholesterol Test Is Useful. But It Does Not Show Every Heart Risk.** | RECOGNISE · CHOLESTEROL TESTING | **What does the standard cholesterol test show, and what remains outside it?** | Explain why the standard panel is an invaluable foundation but not a complete cardiovascular risk assessment by design, then open the logical transition into Clarify. |

The four resources may be presented in this order, but they are not publicly numbered.

Readers may follow the recommended route or enter wherever their concern begins.

## 3. Public identity

Lead with the compelling resource title, not its shelf position.

### Preferred title treatment

```text
# The Heart We Are Trying to Protect

RECOGNISE · THE HEART

How the heart, circulation, and blood work together, and why cardiovascular risk is rarely one isolated problem.

THE QUESTION
What are we trying to protect?
```

The visible hierarchy is:

1. title;
2. stage and topic kicker;
3. one-sentence deck;
4. the central question;
5. useful metadata.

Do not include:

- “Briefing” in the public title;
- a public sequence number;
- “Chapter” unless the resource is shown inside a compiled collection;
- an invented proprietary method name.

## 4. “The question” component

The question each resource addresses is informative for readers and should be visible.

### On the Recognise hub

Each core resource card should include:

- title;
- kicker or topic;
- one-sentence promise;
- **The question**;
- reading time;
- format availability;
- a clear link.

Example:

> **The Heart We Are Trying to Protect**  
> How the heart, circulation, and blood work together.  
> **The question:** What are we trying to protect?

### On the resource page

Place the question close to the title and deck, before the At a glance section.

It may appear as a restrained editorial block:

> **THE QUESTION**  
> What are we trying to protect?

### In PDF and print

Include the question on the opening page beneath the deck.

### In metadata

Use:

```yaml
resource_question:
```

The question is not a quiz, assessment, or promise of a personalised answer. It is the organising question the resource explores and answers at a general educational level.

## 5. The six honest and true friends

Every Recognise resource must answer, explicitly or implicitly:

1. **What?** What is the subject, problem, process, or risk?
2. **Where?** Where does it occur, act, appear, or become relevant?
3. **When?** When does it matter, develop, appear, change, or require action?
4. **Why?** Why does it matter to health, risk, decisions, or prevention?
5. **Who?** Who may be affected, who should pay attention, and who should interpret or act?
6. **How?** How does it work, become recognised, get discussed, or lead to a next step?

These are mandatory editorial checks, not six compulsory visible headings.

The narrative should follow the subject naturally. A concise six-friend summary may appear near the end to make the resource easier to scan and compare.

---

## 6. The “plus” questions

The six friends are strengthened by five additional questions:

### So what?

What should the reader understand differently after reading?

### What does this not mean?

Which common overinterpretations, assumptions, or false conclusions must be prevented?

### What remains uncertain?

Where is the evidence incomplete, context-dependent, evolving, or unsuitable for personal interpretation?

### What is worth discussing?

Which questions may be useful to take to a health team?

### What next?

Which free resource, paid product, health-team conversation, or other route is genuinely relevant next?

Together, these form the HeartFirst Recognise editorial test:

> **What → Where → When → Why → Who → How → Limits → Meaning → Questions → Next**

## 7. Standard content anatomy

Every core Recognise foundation should normally contain the following.

### A. Publication identity

- compelling title;
- Recognise stage and topic kicker;
- one-sentence deck;
- **The question**;
- reading time;
- publication date;
- last-reviewed date;
- version;
- available formats;
- audience or intended use, where helpful.

### B. At a glance

Three to five concise points that give the reader immediate value.

The section should answer:

- What is this?
- Why does it matter?
- What should I carry forward?

### C. Key takeaway

One memorable, evidence-compatible statement.

It should be strong enough to remember and careful enough to remain true outside the surrounding paragraph.

### D. Why this matters

Explain practical significance without fear inflation.

### E. Main explanation

Use natural headings that fit the subject.

The main explanation should:

- define essential terms at first use;
- keep related but clinically distinct concepts separate;
- distinguish mechanism, association, evidence, and interpretation;
- use examples only when they clarify;
- avoid implying that every fact applies equally to every reader;
- answer the resource’s central question clearly.

### F. Six-friend summary

A concise summary panel answering what, where, when, why, who, and how.

### G. What this does not mean

Correct likely overinterpretations.

Examples:

- one reassuring result does not establish that all risk is absent;
- one risk factor does not predict an event;
- an educational resource cannot determine which tests or treatments a person needs;
- feeling well does not answer every risk question;
- uncertainty is not the same as danger;
- a test is not deficient merely because it was not designed to assess another domain.

### H. What this means for you

Translate the subject into realistic, non-prescriptive significance.

Prefer:

- recognise;
- record;
- check;
- prepare;
- discuss;
- take forward;
- ask;
- clarify.

Avoid:

- diagnose;
- determine;
- prove;
- guarantee;
- demand;
- prescribe;
- optimise a personal treatment plan.

### I. Questions worth considering

Questions should help the reader organise thoughts and prepare conversations.

Do not present them as questions every person must ask.

### J. Where to go next

Offer several legitimate routes, such as:

- another Recognise foundation;
- a supporting Recognise resource;
- a glossary definition;
- a research review;
- a health-team conversation;
- Clarify, Navigate, Prevent, or Protect;
- return to the Recognise hub.

Do not force every resource into the same sales call to action.

### K. Sources and review information

Show:

- principal authoritative or primary sources;
- publication and review dates;
- version;
- editorial responsibility;
- clinical or expert review status, where applicable;
- correction contact;
- material limitations.

## 8. The four-foundation relationship

The foundations should connect without collapsing into one another.

### Foundation 1: the system

Explains what the heart and cardiovascular system are.

### Foundation 2: internal risk

Explains risks and disease processes within the body.

### Foundation 3: lived exposure

Explains lifestyle and environmental conditions that may influence risk and internal processes.

### Foundation 4: the familiar baseline

Explains what the standard cholesterol test contributes and what it does not establish.

The intended progression is:

> **The system → internal strain → lived exposure → familiar baseline → Clarify what is known and unknown**

This progression should be visible on the hub and in the closing routes of each foundation.

---

## 9. Special standard for the cholesterol-testing foundation

The fourth foundation must reconcile two truths:

1. The standard lipid panel is invaluable, scalable, familiar, and tied to proven prevention and treatment decisions.
2. It is not a complete cardiovascular risk assessment by design.

The resource should distinguish:

### Information commonly included in the panel

Usually:

- total cholesterol;
- HDL cholesterol;
- triglycerides;
- calculated or measured LDL cholesterol;
- often calculated non-HDL cholesterol.

Use **usually includes** because composition and reporting vary by country, laboratory, and clinical context.

### Lipid information outside the usual panel

Examples may include:

- Lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a));
- ApoB, unless separately ordered;
- particle discordance or specialised lipoprotein information.

### Wider cardiovascular information outside the panel’s purpose

Examples include:

- blood pressure;
- glucose regulation and diabetes status;
- kidney function;
- smoking and nicotine exposure;
- family history;
- symptoms;
- rhythm;
- valve or structural disease;
- plaque imaging;
- lifestyle and environmental context;
- established diagnoses;
- selected inflammatory or clotting information.

Do not say that every risk outside the panel is hidden.

Prefer:

> **A standard lipid panel is not a complete cardiovascular risk assessment by design. It provides important lipid information, but it does not establish whether other relevant risks have been measured, recorded, connected, or discussed.**

Its closing question should lead directly into Clarify:

> **What is already known, and what remains unknown or incomplete?**

---

## 10. Lifestyle and environmental standard

The third foundation must avoid a morality-based treatment of lifestyle.

It should recognise that:

- not every exposure is chosen;
- not every modifiable risk is easily modifiable;
- people do not have equal control over work, income, housing, air quality, safe movement, food access, stress, caregiving demands, or access to health care;
- lifestyle and environmental exposures may influence internal processes without becoming the same category.

Use language that supports agency without assigning blame.

---

## 11. Layered reading

Recognise should serve readers with different amounts of time and background knowledge.

### First layer: At a glance

Fast orientation.

### Second layer: Main resource

The complete plain-language explanation.

### Third layer: Go deeper

Optional mechanisms, evidence notes, definitions, and links to research.

## 12. Resource types

### Foundational guide

A substantive explanation of one of the four core questions.  
Typical length: **1,200–2,200 words**

### At a Glance

A one-page summary derived from a longer resource.  
Typical length: **250–500 words**

### Quick guide

Practical orientation or preparation.  
Typical length: **600–1,200 words**

### FAQ and route finder

Question-led answers with clear next destinations.

### Worksheet

Creates a user-owned record or starting point.

### Health-team adaptation

A concise version intended to help a professional understand what information a customer has prepared. It must not instruct clinical practice or imply endorsement.

## 13. Safety and disclaimer boundary

The full **Before You Begin: Safety Check** is not a Recognise foundation and should not be inserted as a major module inside the Recognise collection.

It belongs in the front matter of:

- Clarify;
- Navigate;
- Prevent.

Recognise may retain the ordinary sitewide medical disclaimer, footer emergency link, and standard legal pages appropriate to public health information.

This separation keeps Recognise focused while ensuring that foreseeable product misuse is interrupted at the point where a person may otherwise use a paid tool as a substitute for care.

## 16. Writing standards

Use UK/International English.

Prefer:

- short paragraphs;
- active, direct sentences;
- familiar words before technical terminology;
- full term at first mention;
- clear distinctions;
- calm seriousness;
- uncertainty expressed honestly;
- “health team” without a hyphen;
- “heart risk” without a hyphen;
- “early action” rather than “earlier action”.

Avoid:

- alarmist language;
- false urgency;
- motivational clichés;
- generic reassurance;
- inflated claims of uniqueness;
- unnecessary em dashes;
- medical-sounding authority the resource has not earned;
- implying that reading creates protection by itself;
- implying that every risk interacts with every other risk;
- treating lifestyle as a measure of virtue;
- excessive repetition of the HeartFirst system.

## 18. Accessibility requirements

Every resource should support:

- semantic heading order;
- keyboard navigation;
- visible focus;
- sufficient contrast;
- readable body width;
- zoom and reflow;
- screen-reader-friendly link text;
- accessible tables;
- text alternatives for diagrams;
- reduced-motion preferences;
- clean print and PDF output;
- a no-colour-only meaning rule.

The question and six-friend summary should remain readable as linear text on small screens and for assistive technology.

---

## 19. Evidence and boundary rules

Before publication, classify every substantive claim as:

- anatomy or physiology;
- mechanism;
- association;
- population statistic;
- guideline recommendation;
- clinical outcome;
- emerging evidence;
- editorial interpretation.

Then confirm:

- appropriate source quality;
- wording proportional to the evidence;
- publication and review date;
- whether qualification is needed;
- whether the claim belongs in Recognise.

Recognise may explain medical science. It must not convert general evidence into personalised assessment.

---

## 20. Publication quality gate

A Recognise resource is ready only when the answer to every relevant question is yes.

### Purpose

- Does it have one clear job?
- Does it answer one central public question?
- Does it belong in Recognise?
- Is its next route legitimate?

### Six friends

- Does it adequately answer what, where, when, why, who, and how?

### Value

- Can a reader gain something useful from At a glance alone?
- Does the full resource add genuine depth?
- Is there a practical carry-forward point?

### Safety

- Are diagnostic and treatment boundaries clear?
- Have likely misinterpretations been addressed?
- Does it avoid fear, coercion, shame, blame, and false reassurance?

### Evidence

- Are factual claims sourced and current?
- Are mechanism, association, and clinical meaning distinguished?
- Are uncertainties visible?

### Experience

- Is the title compelling?
- Is the central question visible?
- Is the reading path clear?
- Is the resource accessible on web, mobile, print, and PDF?
- Is the next route easy to find?

### Governance

- Are version, publication date, last review, and correction details present?
- Does the canonical web page remain the source of truth?
